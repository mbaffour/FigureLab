❔ Help & FAQ

What is FigureLab?

FigureLab is a single HTML file for assembling publication-quality scientific figures — microscopy, western blots, histology, gels, clinical photos, or any image-based figure. It runs 100% in your browser: no installation, no server, no sign-in.

Your images and data never leave your computer — nothing is uploaded anywhere. Because everything runs locally, it also works completely offline.

60-second quick start

  1. Open figure_lab.html in your browser (Chrome or Firefox recommended).
  2. Drag images onto the drop zone — or click it to browse and pick files.
  3. Set your grid: choose the number of rows and columns in the Layout section.
  4. Adjust panel labels (A/B/C) and turn on scale bars where you need them.
  5. Press R (or click ⟳ Render) to preview the figure.
  6. Set a file name and DPI, then export in your chosen format.
Tip Press R any time you change a setting to refresh the preview. A "render outdated" cue appears when the canvas is behind your latest edits.

The interface at a glance

The window has three zones:

  • Left — the image list and settings. Each image gets its own panel with adjustments (brightness, scale bar, label, crop, and more), plus the Layout section for grid size and gaps.
  • Center — the canvas, where your rendered figure appears. A toolbar above it holds zoom, fit, labels, and annotation tools; an info bar below shows pixel dimensions and physical size in mm.
  • Right / Export — the Export panel for file name, format, and DPI, plus a Figure notes scratchpad and metadata exports.

Core workflow, step by step

1. Add and arrange panels

Drop images to add panels. To reorder them, drag the ⠿ handle in the image list. Locked panels cannot be moved — toggle Lock panel to protect one from accidental drag-reorder. Use ⎘ (Duplicate) to reuse a panel's settings on a similar image.

2. Adjust each panel

Open a panel's settings to fine-tune it with live preview: Brightness, Contrast, and Gamma sliders (gamma is great for dim fluorescence), plus grayscale/invert, black/white points, fluorescence LUTs, rotate, and flip. Apply to all copies brightness, contrast, gamma, LUT, and B/W points to every panel at once.

3. Add a calibrated scale bar

Automatic: when you import a TIFF that carries pixel-size metadata — OME-TIFF, ImageJ, Zeiss LSM, or a centimetre-resolution tag — FigureLab reads the µm/px and calibrates the panel on import (you'll see a toast; print-DPI resolution is ignored so it's never mistaken for a scale). 📎 Read TIFF metadata in a panel re-applies it.

Manual: click 🔬 Calibrate from image in a panel's settings, click two points a known distance apart, and enter the real-world distance — FigureLab computes the µm/px ratio. (Objective presets from 4× to 100× are also available.) Then enable the scale bar; Auto SB picks a clean length automatically.

Tip Pick the bar's unit (nm / µm / mm) next to its length — handy for EM (nm) or gross specimens (mm). You can also drag the left end of the scale bar on the canvas to resize it (it snaps to clean values).
Note An uncalibrated warning badge (⚠) appears on a scale bar when µm/px has not been set. Calibrate first so the bar is accurate.

4. Label the panels

Turn on panel labels and choose a format — A/B/C, a/b/c, 1/2/3, or i/ii/iii — with control over position, size, colour, font, and bold/italic.

5. Space panels apart

Hover the seam between two panels on the canvas: a blue divider with a gap pill appears. Drag it (or tap + / −) to push them apart — horizontal seams control row spacing, vertical seams control column spacing. For independent gaps, tick Per-gutter spacing; Reset gutters returns to a uniform grid.

6. Annotate

Draw directly on the rendered figure with the toolbar tools: arrow, double-headed arrow, ruler, rectangle, ellipse, line, text, inset, and counter. Switch to ↖ Select (or press Esc) to move, resize, restyle, or delete an annotation. Hold Shift while drawing to snap to 45° or force squares and circles.

Tip Annotations are baked into the exported figure, so finalise your layout before annotating. Full undo/redo is available if you change your mind.

7. Export

In the Export panel, set a file name and DPI (72 / 150 / 300 / 600 / 1200), then export as PNG, JPEG, WebP, TIFF, PDF, or SVG. For print and journal submission, use 300–600 DPI — raster exports are re-rendered at the true target-DPI pixel count (supersampled), so text, scale bars, and lines stay crisp. TIFF or lossless PDF at 300–600 DPI on a white background is preferred by most journals.

Saving your work

Save the entire figure — grid, panel settings, calibrations, annotations, figure notes, and the images themselves — as a JSON file (Ctrl+S), and load it again later to pick up exactly where you left off.

Note Session files embed your image pixels, so loading one restores the whole figure — no need to re-drop anything. (Very old sessions saved before this was added will prompt you to re-drop those images.)

1 · Multi-panel microscopy figure

The flagship workflow: assemble several micrographs into a tidy A/B/C grid with matching scale bars, ready for a results figure. Good when you have 3–6 related images that belong in one composite.

  1. Drag all your images onto the drop zone (or click to browse). Each becomes a panel.
  2. In the Layout section, set rows × columns to your target grid (e.g. 1 × 3 for A/B/C, or 2 × 2). Watch the mini grid map to confirm panel assignments.
  3. Reorder panels by dragging the ⠿ handle in the image list until A/B/C run in the right order.
  4. Turn on Panel labels and pick the A/B/C format, then set position, size, and colour.
  5. Calibrate the first panel: open its settings, click 🔬 Calibrate from image, click two known points, and enter the real distance — or choose the matching objective preset (4× – 100×).
  6. Enable the scale bar on that panel and click Auto SB to pick a clean length (≈ 20% of panel width).
  7. Space panels out: hover the seam between two panels until the blue gap divider appears, then drag it (or tap +) to set the gutter.
  8. Press R (or click ⟳ Render) to preview, then use ⊡ Fit to check the whole layout.
Tip Calibrate and dial in one panel, then use Apply to all to copy brightness, contrast, gamma, LUT, and B/W points to every panel at once — a fast way to keep a grid visually consistent.

2 · Fluorescence multi-channel merge

For confocal or widefield fluorescence: import separate channel images, assign colourblind-safe LUTs, tune the intensity range, and composite them into a single merged panel.

  1. Drop your base channel image in as a panel and open its settings.
  2. Assign a colourblind-safe LUT from Fluorescence LUTs — Magenta for the first channel and Green for the second read cleanly for most viewers.
  3. Under Black / White point, raise the black point to kill background haze and lower the white point to stretch the signal without clipping real structure.
  4. For dim staining, nudge the Gamma correction slider to lift mid-tones without blowing out bright puncta.
  5. Add the second channel with Add channel — it overlays with an independent LUT using a screen blend, so co-localised signal reads white. New channels default to colourblind-safe LUTs automatically.
  6. Repeat black/white-point tuning per channel until the merge is balanced.
  7. Calibrate with 🔬 Calibrate from image, enable the scale bar, and press R to render.
Tip Magenta + green (rather than red + green) keeps overlap visible as white and stays readable for red–green colourblind reviewers — a common journal accessibility ask.

3 · Western blot / gel panel

For blots and gels where you need clean grayscale lanes, honest contrast, and a lossless print-ready export. Use when preparing a loading-control or knockdown figure.

  1. Drop the scanned blot in and open its panel settings.
  2. Toggle Grayscale on. If your scan is white-bands-on-black, use Invert to show dark bands on a white ground.
  3. Trim to the lanes you need with Per-panel crop — set edge percentages or use the interactive crop editor.
  4. Adjust Brightness and Contrast only enough to make bands clear; keep it linear and even across the whole image.
  5. Press R to render, then label with the Text tool (molecular weights, lanes) and point at a band of interest with the Arrow tool.
  6. Set a file name and DPI of 300–600, then export as TIFF or PDF (lossless) for a lossless submission file.
Tip Keep adjustments uniform across the entire blot — cropping a lane out or applying local contrast can be flagged as image manipulation. Annotations are baked into the export, so finalise the crop and levels before you label.

4 · Histology / brightfield panel with annotations

For H&E, IHC, or other brightfield sections where you want to call out features, add a magnified inset, and show a calibrated scale bar.

  1. Drop your section image in and set brightness/contrast so the stain colours read true.
  2. Calibrate: click 🔬 Calibrate from image and enter a known distance, or pick the objective preset used at the scope. Enable the scale bar and click Auto SB.
  3. Press R to render, then draw a magnified region with the Inset tool — it magnifies the region and drops it into a panel corner automatically.
  4. Point at structures with the Arrow tool; hold Shift while drawing to snap to 45° increments.
  5. Add labels with the Text tool, or double-click the panel to type a pinned label with a readability halo over the image.
  6. Fine-tune anything: press Esc to enter ↖ Select mode, click an annotation, and reposition or restyle it from the floating toolbar. Use Ctrl+Z to undo.
Tip Use 📌 Panel annotations mode so arrows and insets pin to their panel — they follow the image if you later reorder panels, so callouts never drift onto the wrong section.

5 · Before/after or time-series row

For a single row that tells a story across conditions or timepoints (0 h → 24 h → 48 h, or control → treated). The key is identical adjustments on every panel plus clear headers.

  1. Drop all timepoint/condition images in and set the Layout to 1 × N so they form one row.
  2. Drag the ⠿ handles to order them left-to-right in time or treatment sequence.
  3. Adjust brightness, contrast, gamma, and B/W points on the first panel only.
  4. Click Apply to all to push those exact settings to every panel — essential so intensity differences reflect biology, not editing.
  5. Turn on Axis labels to add column headers (e.g. "0 h", "24 h", "48 h") and an optional row header for the condition.
  6. Enable a scale bar on the first panel and use Auto SB; add labels via Panel labels if the row also needs A/B/C.
  7. Press R to render and ⊡ Fit to review the sequence.
Tip If one panel needs its own breathing room, tick Per-gutter spacing in the Layout panel and drag just that gutter; Reset gutters restores a uniform row.

6 · Preparing a journal submission figure

The final-mile checklist: match a journal's spec, export a lossless high-resolution file, and generate the caption plus reproducibility records your methods section needs.

  1. Apply a Journal preset (Nature, Science, Cell, or Poster) for one-click sizing that fits the target.
  2. Confirm a white background and that scale bars are calibrated — any ⚠ uncalibrated warning badge on a scale bar must be resolved first.
  3. Set a clear file name and choose DPI of 300–600. Check the info bar below the canvas for the physical size in mm at that DPI.
  4. Export as TIFF (uncompressed, pixel count scaled to the true DPI) or PDF (lossless) — journals prefer these over JPEG.
  5. Add a short note to each image in its settings, then click ✦ Generate Caption to produce a structured A/B/C caption with scale-bar text.
  6. Click ⬇ CSV Metadata to export every panel's settings (µm/px, scale bar, brightness, gamma, crop, notes) for your methods and lab records.
  7. Export an R script (magick) or Python script (Pillow + matplotlib) so anyone can recreate the figure from the original image files.
  8. Press Ctrl+S to save the session JSON (images included) so you can reopen and revise the whole figure later.
Tip The reproducibility script plus the CSV metadata together document exactly how each panel was processed — pasting the key values into your methods section pre-empts the "how were images adjusted?" reviewer question.

Privacy & data

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. FigureLab runs entirely in your browser — everything happens on your own computer and no image or setting ever leaves it. There is no server, no account, and no telemetry. You can even work fully offline (see the browser question below).

Does FigureLab work offline?

Yes. The whole tool is a single HTML file, so once it is open it needs no internet connection. The only thing fetched online is the display fonts (JetBrains Mono and Instrument Serif) from Google Fonts — if you are offline these simply fall back to your system fonts and nothing else changes.

Tip To guarantee an offline copy, just save figure_lab.html locally and open it directly.
Which browsers work best?

Chrome and Firefox are recommended and give the most reliable canvas rendering and export behaviour. Other Chromium-based browsers generally work too, but if you hit an export or clipboard quirk, try Chrome first.

Is it free? Is it open source?

Yes — FigureLab is free and open source under the MIT licence. You may use, modify, and redistribute it, including for commercial and academic work.

Images & formats

What image formats can I import?

Drag any common raster image the browser can decode onto the drop zone (or click to browse) — microscopy exports, western blots, histology, gels, or clinical photos. FigureLab automatically reads µm/px scale calibration from OME-TIFF, ImageJ, Zeiss LSM and cm-resolution TIFF metadata on import, so a calibrated scale bar is ready with no clicking (you can always override it).

What can I export, and which format should I use?

Pick the format that matches where the figure is going:

  • PNG — lossless raster with true DPI embedded; a safe default for most submissions.
  • TIFF — uncompressed RGB scaled to your target DPI; preferred by most journals.
  • PDF / PDF (lossless) — embedded raster at the correct physical page size; use lossless for line art and blots.
  • SVG — background raster plus your annotations as native vector elements (labels and scale bars stay razor-sharp).
  • JPEG — smallest file, but lossy; fine for drafts and slides, not for line art.
  • WebP — a good size/quality balance for the web.
How do I make a multi-channel fluorescence merge?

Open a panel's settings and use Add channel to layer extra images, each with its own fluorescence LUT (Fire, Green, Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Blue); the channels are combined with a screen blend. New channels default to a colourblind-safe magenta / green pairing rather than red / green, so overlap reads white and the merge stays readable for viewers with red-green colour vision deficiency.

My fluorescence image is too dim to see — what should I adjust?

Use the per-panel Gamma slider for nonlinear brightening that lifts dim signal without blowing out bright areas, and set the Black / White point to clip and stretch the useful intensity range. Brightness and Contrast are also available with live preview. Use Apply to all to copy these settings to every panel for consistency.

Scale bars & calibration

Why does my scale bar say "uncalibrated" (⚠)?

The warning badge appears whenever a panel's µm/px ratio has not been set, so FigureLab cannot know the real-world length of the bar. Calibrate the panel and the badge disappears.

How do I calibrate a panel?

Two ways:

  • Two-point calibration — click 🔬 Calibrate from image in the panel settings, click two points a known distance apart (e.g. a stage micrometer), and enter the real distance. FigureLab computes µm/px automatically.
  • Objective preset — pick your objective (4×–100×) to apply an approximate µm/px value.
Tip Once calibrated, press Auto SB to snap the bar to a clean length (about 20% of the panel width).
Can I match the physical scale across several panels?

Yes. After calibrating, use the scale-matching option so panels imaged at the same magnification share one consistent µm/px, keeping bar lengths comparable across the figure.

Export & print quality

What DPI should I use for journal submission?

Use 300 DPI for photographic / greyscale images and 600–1200 DPI for line art and combination figures (charts, blots with sharp text). The DPI selector offers 72 / 150 / 300 / 600 / 1200. FigureLab re-renders the figure at the true target-DPI pixel count (supersampled), so text, lines, and scale bars stay crisp — it is not merely tagging the DPI.

Tip The export health check warns you if you try to export below 300 DPI.
What is the difference between PDF and PDF (lossless)?

The standard PDF embeds the figure as a JPEG-compressed raster — small, but lossy, which can add ringing artefacts around sharp edges. A lossless PDF uses Flate (non-lossy) compression instead. Prefer lossless for line art, western blots, and sharp text, where JPEG artefacts are visible and some journals reject JPEG-in-PDF for line art. For photographic panels the standard PDF is usually fine.

My exported PNG opens at the wrong size or DPI in Photoshop / ImageJ — why?

This is fixed. FigureLab now writes a standard pHYs resolution chunk into the PNG, which is what Photoshop, GIMP, and ImageJ/FIJI actually read for physical resolution. Older versions embedded the DPI only as a text field, so editors reported the browser default (72 DPI) at a giant pixel size. Re-export and the true DPI will be reported correctly.

The figure looks blurry when zoomed or after export — what do I do?

Export at a higher DPI (300–600, or 1200 for line art): raster exports are re-rendered at the real pixel count, not upscaled, so more DPI means genuinely more detail. On screen, FigureLab keeps a separate high-resolution preview layer matched to your display (HiDPI/Retina) so labels and annotations stay sharp when you zoom in — the underlying measurement buffer is left untouched so quantification stays exact.

Tip Very large renders are capped at about 60 megapixels to stay within browser memory; if you hit the cap, a toast tells you and the export is scaled down rather than crashing.
How do I get a clean white background for print?

For journal submission, use TIFF at 300–600 DPI with a white background. You can also press Ctrl + P for a print-ready view that hides all the app chrome.

Saving & reproducibility

Do my images get saved in the session JSON?

Yes — the session file embeds your image pixel data along with the layout, per-panel settings, annotations, and figure notes, so loading it restores the entire figure with no re-dropping. Everything still stays on your computer; the JSON is just a local file you save yourself. (Sessions saved by very old versions, before pixel data was embedded, will prompt you to re-drop those images.)

How do I save and reload a figure?

Save the whole figure as a JSON file with Ctrl + S (or the Save button), and load it later to restore the grid, adjustments, annotations, and images. The Figure notes scratchpad in the Export panel is also stored in the session — handy for antibody dilutions or reviewer comments.

Can I reproduce a figure programmatically?

Yes. Export an R script (using magick) or a Python script (using Pillow + matplotlib) that recreates the figure from your original image files. You can also export CSV metadata listing every panel's name, label, µm/px, scale bar, brightness, gamma, crop, and caption — useful for methods sections and lab records.

Can FigureLab write my figure caption?

Add a short note to each image in its settings, then click ✦ Generate Caption to assemble a structured, journal-style caption with panel letters and scale-bar lengths — e.g. "Figure. (A) Control cells. Scale bar, 10µm. (B) Treated cells. Scale bar, 10µm." Edit the result to taste.

What is the "on-device AI" caption polish, and is it private?

Optional. After generating a caption you can click 🤖 Polish with on-device AI to have it rewritten more fluently by Chrome's built-in Gemini Nano model. It runs entirely on your own machine — no server, no API key, and nothing is uploaded, so it keeps FigureLab's privacy promise. If your browser doesn't support the built-in model, FigureLab just keeps the standard rule-based caption. It needs a recent Chrome (138+) with built-in AI; the model downloads once and then works offline.

Tip The AI only rewrites wording — it's instructed never to invent results and to keep your panel letters and scale-bar values exactly. Always read the output before using it.

Troubleshooting

My annotations moved or look wrong after I changed the layout.

Annotations are baked into the exported figure, so finalise your grid and panel order before annotating. To keep an annotation tied to a specific panel, draw it in 📌 panel mode — it will follow that panel if you reorder. Full Undo / Redo (Ctrl+Z / Ctrl+Y) covers layout, per-panel adjustments, and annotations, one step per edit.

A panel won't drag to reorder.

It is probably locked. Locked panels cannot be dragged (this prevents accidental reordering). Unlock the panel, then drag the ⠿ handle in the image list to move it.

How should I cite FigureLab?

FigureLab is archived on Zenodo with a citable DOI. Use the concept DOI — it always resolves to the latest version:

Awuah, M. B. (2026). FigureLab: a browser-based tool for assembling publication-quality scientific figures. Zenodo. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21269456

To cite the exact version you used, use its version DOI instead — e.g. v3.7.0 = 10.5281/zenodo.21466208. The repository also ships a CITATION.cff, so GitHub shows a "Cite this repository" button with ready-made APA & BibTeX metadata.
